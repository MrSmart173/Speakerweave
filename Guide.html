SpeakerWeave

One sound source in, as many sound outputs as you like out, arranged as one surround system.

Every channel of every output (the left speaker of the left TV, the right speaker of the middle monitor, a subwoofer on a sound card and so on) is treated as its own speaker. You give each one a role (front left, side right, rear left, subwoofer ...), place it on a map of your room, and shape its sound.

What it can do

  • Modes from 2.0 up to 7.1 (2.0, 2.1, 3.0, 3.1, 4.0, 4.1, 5.0, 5.1, 6.0, 6.1, 7.0, 7.1)
  • 4.0, 4.1, 5.0 and 5.1 with the surround pair at your sides (SL SR) or behind you (RL RR)
  • Custom: tick any combination of positions, with up to four subwoofers
  • Any channel of any output can play any role. Several speakers may share a role
  • Room map: drag screens, loose speakers and your own seat to where they really are
  • Drag the rim of a single speaker to move it along one direction, with an optional Mirror mode that moves its partner on the other side to match
  • Roles can be filled in for you from the positions on the map
  • Screens with no speakers of their own: mark the output as "separate speakers plugged into its aux, headphone or line out" and place those speakers freely
  • Frequency limits per speaker (for example a subwoofer that only plays 10 to 75 Hz), with ready made speaker types from tweeters to bass shakers
  • A built in library of common TVs, monitors, speaker systems, studio monitors, headsets and sound cards. Recognised outputs get a matching preset as soon as you tick them
  • Test sounds on a single speaker: right click it on the map, or select it and press Space. Add up to five sounds of your own (WAV, MP3, M4A, WMA, FLAC and more)
  • Solo a speaker and every other one shows as muted on the map. Muted speakers hand their part to the speakers still playing, and a soloed speaker plays the whole sound
  • Sweeps that glide a sound round the room (360°) or across the front speakers
  • Now playing: the song or video playing on the PC (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube and more) under the map with play, pause and skip, and a popup that closes itself after 5 seconds
  • Microphones on the room map, placed where they really are, glowing with what they hear
  • A Performance page with SpeakerWeave's share of the processor and memory, how hard the sound work runs, delay, dropouts and a two minute chart
  • Colour themes: six presets, or your own colours, also straight from Adobe Color
  • Plays by itself as soon as it opens, and makes itself the Windows sound output while it plays. Stop it and Windows gets its old output back
  • Sets the virtual sound device to 7.1 by itself, so games send real surround. Music and other stereo sound is spread over every speaker until surround arrives
  • Units with more than two channels (a Logitech Z906, a 5.1 sound card) show as a ring of their own speakers with the subwoofer beside it. Every speaker can be attached to its unit or loose, and switching back and forth keeps the loose places
  • Level meters like a mixer: one block for the PC sound and one per output, with a bar for every channel
  • An Errors page that lists anything keeping the sound from your speakers or spoiling the surround, each with a button that fixes it. The Errors button flashes red for an error and lights yellow for a new warning, and a note at startup sums up what is wrong
  • Bass shakers (ButtKicker and other tactile transducers) as a speaker role, limited to the low frequencies and fed by the game's bass, by effects, or both
  • Extra sound sources played on top of the game sound, such as SimHub's ShakeIt effects (tyres, impacts, gear shifts) or a spotter app, each channel sent to the speakers you pick
  • A SimHub plugin: SpeakerWeave loads the speaker setup you chose for each game, and SimHub buttons and dashboards can control SpeakerWeave
  • A tour of the whole window the first time it opens
  • Controls: keys, game controller, racing wheel and button box buttons, and Stream Deck links for volume, mute, modes, setups and more, working while a game is in front
  • Settings: start with Windows, start hidden in the tray, keep running in the tray
  • A list of the Bluetooth devices paired with the PC
  • Parametric equaliser per speaker, up to 16 bands, with a live response graph
  • Volume, delay, mute, solo and polarity per speaker, plus volume and delay per output
  • Bass from every channel can also be sent to the subwoofer
  • Stereo sources can be spread over all speakers, surround sources are folded down to whatever speakers you have, so no channel is ever lost
  • Keeps outputs with different clocks in step with each other
  • RGB lights per speaker through OpenRGB: steady, pulsing with the sound, level meter, rainbow
  • Your setup is saved automatically, and can be saved under a name and opened again

Installing (Windows 10 or 11)

With the installer. Run SpeakerWeave-Setup-Beta-v1.0.exe (or the newest one on the Releases page), choose where to install, and start SpeakerWeave from the Start menu. Nothing else is needed. The app looks for newer versions by itself when it opens and every six hours. When one is out, a note appears by the Errors button and the Errors page lists it under Notices, with what's new and a button that installs it. Setup > Check for updates looks right away.

From this folder, for development.

  1. Install Python 3.12 or newer from https://www.python.org/downloads/ and tick Add python.exe to PATH on the first page of the installer.
  2. Double click Install.bat. It downloads the libraries into this folder only.
  3. Start the app with SpeakerWeave.bat.

If the app does not open, start SpeakerWeave (show messages).bat instead. It shows the error text. Errors are also written to error log.txt in %LOCALAPPDATA%\SpeakerWeave.

To build the installer yourself, see RELEASING.md.

First steps: map your speakers

The first time SpeakerWeave opens, a short tour shows where everything is. Take it again any time from Setup > Take the tour.

You can do all of this before setting up anything else, using the built in test signal.

  1. In the list on the left, tick every sound output you want to use (your three TVs, a sound card and so on). Each one appears on the room map.
  2. Pick the Mode at the top (for example 5.1), or press Positions to tick exactly the speaker positions your room has (see below).
  3. Drag the screens and the You marker to where they are in your room. Scroll over a screen to turn it. The faint ring around you shows the ideal spot for each role.
  4. Press Set roles, or click each speaker and choose its Role by hand.
  5. Leave Listen to on Test signal and press Start. A noise walks from channel to channel, and the speaker that should be playing gets a white ring on the map. If the wrong speaker makes the noise, change its role. Identify (or a double click on the map) plays a noise on one speaker only.

Hiding outputs you don't use

Right click an output in the list and choose Hide this output. It moves into Hidden by user, a folder at the bottom of the list, and leaves the map, every saved setup and the Errors page alone. Its place on the map and its settings are kept. Right click it there and choose Show this output again to bring it back as it was.

When a hidden output is plugged in again, the folder flashes and a note says the device has been found again: Activate device brings it back ticked, Ignore leaves it hidden and asks again next time, and Ignore and don't show again never asks about it again.

Side or rear surround, and custom positions

4.0, 4.1, 5.0 and 5.1 appear twice in the Mode list: once with the surround pair beside you (side pair SL SR) and once behind you (rear pair RL RR). Switching between the two moves the speakers of that pair along, so you do not have to set their roles again. A 5.1 source played on a rear pair setup sends its surround channels to the rear speakers.

For anything else, choose Custom... at the bottom of the Mode list or press Positions. Tick any combination of front left, centre, front right, side left, side right, rear left, rear centre and rear right, and choose 0 to 4 subwoofers. A choice that matches a standard mode becomes that mode, everything else is shown as Custom with the usual numbering (for example Custom 4.2 for four speakers and two subwoofers). Sound meant for a position you do not have is folded into the nearest positions you do have, so nothing goes missing. Every subwoofer plays the same bass, and each one keeps its own delay and equaliser.

Moving single speakers and Mirror

Hover over the rim of a speaker on the map and four small arrows appear. Drag the left or right rim to move only that speaker sideways, or the top or bottom rim to move it only forward and back. For a screen with built in speakers this stretches and turns the screen while its other end stays where it is.

Press Mirror under the map and the partner speaker follows: the opposite way sideways, the same way front to back. So a sideways drag changes the spacing evenly on both sides, and a front to back drag moves both ends together.

The numbers under Position in the room follow while you drag, on the Output tab for a whole screen and on the Speaker tab for a single speaker. You can also type exact values there.

Your example from the request, three TVs in a row, looks like this in mode 6.0:

Output Left channel Right channel
Left TV Rear left Side left
Middle TV Front left Front right
Right TV Side right Rear right

Playing the sound of your PC through it

SpeakerWeave needs one place to listen to: a virtual sound device that Windows plays into, and that makes no sound by itself.

  1. Install the free VB CABLE driver (the SpeakerWeave installer offers it, or get it from https://vb-audio.com/Cable/) and restart the PC.
  2. Open SpeakerWeave and tick your outputs. That is all.

When SpeakerWeave opens it renames VB CABLE's output (CABLE Input) to SpeakerWeave Virtual Speaker output, so Windows' lists say what it is for (Windows adds "VB-Audio Virtual Cable" in brackets itself). It listens to it, sets it to 7.1 so games send real surround channels, makes it the Windows sound output and starts the sound. Everything Windows plays then arrives in SpeakerWeave and is spread over your speakers. Press Stop, or quit, and Windows goes back to the output it used before.

While only the two front channels carry sound (music, most videos), SpeakerWeave spreads them over every speaker. As soon as a game sends surround, each channel goes to its own speaker. The Errors page shows which of the two is happening right now.

If a game or app lets you choose its sound output, choose SpeakerWeave Virtual Speaker output. Uninstalling SpeakerWeave gives the device its own name back, and Call the virtual device "SpeakerWeave Virtual Speaker output" on the Settings page switches the renaming off.

Now playing

When music or a video plays on the PC (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube in a browser, or any app that Windows' own media controls show), its title, artist and picture appear in a strip under the map, with previous, play or pause, and next. When a new song or video starts, a small popup shows it in the corner of the screen. It closes itself after 5 seconds (it waits while the mouse is on it), or straight away with its X. Both can be switched off on the Settings page under Now playing.

Each step can be switched off on the Settings page under Windows sound output, and Listen to at the top can be set by hand. You can listen to a normal output device instead of a virtual one, but then that device still plays the original sound itself and cannot be used as part of the setup. A recording device (line in, a capture card, a microphone) can be the source as well.

Units with more than two channels

A unit with more than two channels, such as a Logitech Z906, a 5.1 or 7.1 sound card or an AV receiver, shows on the map as a ring of its own speakers in their surround places, with its subwoofer off to the left. When you tick it, the mode follows its channels (5.1 for six, 7.1 for eight). Drag the ring to move the whole unit, and set how big it is with Size across on the Output tab.

Select a unit and press Speakers attached at the top right of the map (or right click it) to switch to Speakers loose. Every speaker can then be dragged on its own. Press it again and the speakers snap back to the unit, while the unit stays where it is. Loosen them once more and they return to the places you gave them before. This works the same for a screen or soundbar with its two speakers.

Some outputs tell Windows they are 7.1 (or 5.1) when they are not: the Logitech G560, gaming headsets whose software turns on virtual surround (Logitech G HUB, Corsair iCUE, Razer Synapse), and PC soundbars such as the Creative Katana V2. When SpeakerWeave recognises one by its name, it shows what is really there: a headset's two sides, or two speakers and a subwoofer, in front of you. The mode stays as it was and the other channels are left silent. To use every channel Windows offers anyway, set Speaker model on the Output tab to Not chosen. A TV, receiver or sound card keeps every channel: when those offer 5.1 or 7.1, the speakers are really there.

Level meters

Levels under the map shows meters like the mixer in OBS: the first block is the PC sound coming in, and every ticked output has its own block with a bar for each channel, labelled L and R, or FL, FR, C, SUB and so on for surround. Green is comfortable, yellow is loud, red is close to clipping. The thin line above each bar holds the latest peak for a moment.

Errors

The Errors button at the top shows a number when something is wrong. While there is an error it flashes red (it stays steady while the Errors page is open). A new warning lights it yellow for a second, then it goes back to your colours. When SpeakerWeave opens with something wrong, a note under the button sums it up, with Go to Errors or Ignore. The page lists everything that keeps the sound from your speakers (no virtual device, Windows playing somewhere else, an output unplugged, a device refusing to open) and everything that spoils the surround (the virtual device set to stereo, missing speaker positions), each with a button that fixes it where SpeakerWeave can. Run a full sound check sends a silent test tone through the whole chain, from Windows to every speaker, and reports where it stops.

Bass shakers and effects

Give a ButtKicker or any other tactile transducer the role Bass shaker (Speaker tab, or right click it on the map). It is limited to 10 to 80 Hz, plays no part in the speaker mode, and keeps its role when you change modes. Picking a ButtKicker or Dayton model on the Output tab does this for you.

The Effects tab sets what the shakers feel: the game's bass (with its own level), the effects sent to them, or both.

Sending effects and other sounds to chosen speakers

Games mix all their sounds into one before Windows gets them, so tyres or the spotter cannot be pulled back out of the game sound. What can be sent to chosen speakers is sound that arrives separately:

  • SimHub's ShakeIt effects. SimHub makes them from the game's data (tyre slip, road texture, impacts, gear shifts, engine). In SimHub, open ShakeIt Bass shakers, choose a second virtual device as its output (for example VB CABLE A, or VoiceMeeter), and give each effect group its own channel.
  • A spotter, voice chat or music app. In Windows, open Settings > Sound > Volume mixer and set that app's output to a virtual device.

On the Effects tab press Add a source, choose the device it plays into, and send each of its channels to any speakers and shakers, with its own name and level. Give it 8 channels sets a virtual device to 7.1, so ShakeIt can use eight separate channels. All of it plays on top of the game sound, so a speaker can play the game, the spotter and the tyres at the same time.

SimHub

SpeakerWeave comes with a plugin for SimHub (https://www.simhubdash.com):

  1. Open the Effects tab and press Install the SimHub plugin. Close SimHub first, and say yes when Windows asks to allow the change (SimHub lives in Program Files).
  2. Start SimHub and say yes when it asks to enable the SpeakerWeave plugin.

The Effects tab then shows Connected. Every game SimHub has seen gets a line under Speaker setup for each game: choose a saved setup for it, or set your speakers up for that game and press Use current. When SimHub sees the game start, SpeakerWeave loads that setup with its speakers, volumes and effects. No game running sets the setup for when you leave the game. Changes you make while a game runs are kept in that game's setup.

In SimHub, Controls and events lists SpeakerWeave's actions (start or stop, volume, mute, modes, bass, setup slots) for wheel and button box buttons, and dashboards can show SpeakerWeave's state through properties such as SpeakerWeavePlugin.VolumeDb, SpeakerWeavePlugin.Muted and SpeakerWeavePlugin.Mode.

The plugin only sends something when the game changes and asks for SpeakerWeave's state every two seconds, so it costs SimHub nothing noticeable. It talks to SpeakerWeave on this PC only, with the secret code from SpeakerWeave's settings.

Screens whose sound comes out of an aux jack

Windows sees a TV or monitor as one sound output, whether the sound leaves through its own speakers or through its headphone or aux jack. So:

  1. Tick the screen in the list like any other output.
  2. Click it on the map, open the Output tab and set Where the sound comes out to Separate speakers plugged into its aux, headphone or line out.
  3. The two speakers come loose from the screen on the map. Drag each one to where it stands.

If a monitor has neither speakers nor an audio jack, Windows usually does not list it as a sound output at all, and then there is nothing for the app to send sound to.

Performance

The Performance page shows how much of the PC SpeakerWeave uses: its share of the processor and of the memory, how much of each block's time the sound work takes (above 70% the sound can start to break up), the delay SpeakerWeave adds, dropouts, and how busy the PC is as a whole. A chart shows the last two minutes (point at it to read a moment), and a list shows how every output is keeping up.

Microphones

Every microphone of the PC is listed under Microphones at the bottom of the list on the left. Tick one and it appears on the room map in front of you: drag it to where it really is around your setup. It glows with what it hears, and clicking it shows how far it is from you and in which direction. Right click it to rename it, untick it, or take it off the map. Only the microphones you tick are opened, and only to show their level: nothing is recorded.

Hearing one speaker

Right click a speaker on the map and choose a short noise or a sweep from low to high, or select it and press Space, or use Test sound. This works even while the sound is stopped: SpeakerWeave starts it for the test and stops it again. The sweep is the easiest way to hear where a speaker's frequency limits are.

Test sound also has two sweeps that use every speaker:

  • Sweep round the room (360°): a sound goes round you clockwise from straight ahead, twice, gliding from speaker to speaker
  • Sweep from left to right (front speakers): the sound glides across the speakers in front of you and back, twice

A sweep should move smoothly. A jump, a gap or a speaker that stands out points at its place on the map, its volume or its delay. The PC sound waits while a sweep plays.

Mute and solo

Mute takes a speaker out of the system: what it would have played moves to the nearest speakers that are still playing, so nothing goes missing from the music. Solo plays the whole sound on the soloed speakers only (one soloed speaker gets everything, no louder than one channel), which is the quickest way to hear what a single speaker adds.

Your own test sounds

On the Settings page, under Your own test sounds, choose up to five sound files: a voice saying "left", a favourite piece of music, anything. WAV, MP3, M4A, WMA, FLAC and other files Windows can play are accepted. SpeakerWeave keeps its own copy (up to 30 seconds, quiet parts at the start and end trimmed), so the original file can be moved or deleted. Your sounds then appear next to the noise and the sweep wherever you can play a test sound.

Speaker types and the speaker library

On the Speaker tab, Speaker type fills in a sensible frequency range for that kind of speaker: subwoofers, bass shakers, floor standing, 8, 6.5, 5 and 3 inch monitors, soundbars, TV speakers, small desktop and portable speakers, tiny monitor speakers, midrange drivers and tweeters. They are starting points, fine tune them by ear.

SpeakerWeave also recognises many outputs by the name Windows gives them (TVs, monitors, Logitech, Creative, Razer and SteelSeries speaker systems, headsets, AV receivers, soundbars and audio interfaces). When you tick one, its preset is filled in. Speakers on an ordinary audio jack cannot be seen by Windows, so pick their model on the Output tab under Speaker model (Yamaha HS, KRK Rokit, JBL 3 Series, PreSonus Eris, Edifier, Audioengine, Kanto, Mackie, M-Audio, ButtKicker and more, or simply the woofer size).

Controls

The Controls page (top right) gives actions a key or a button:

  • Keys work everywhere, also while a game is in front.
  • Controller, wheel or button box buttons and hat switches, read while a game is in front.
  • Stream Deck: give a Stream Deck key a keyboard shortcut, or switch on links and use the Website action with "GET request in background". Touch Portal, SimHub and similar tools can use the same links.

Actions: start or stop the sound, volume up and down, mute, next and previous mode, bass to the subwoofer, fill every speaker, test signal, lights, show or hide the window, load setup slots 1 to 4, and mute or unmute each output.

Settings

The Settings page has: start with Windows, start hidden in the system tray, start the sound as soon as SpeakerWeave opens (on from the start), keep running in the tray when the window is closed, watch for sound outputs being plugged in, fill in presets for recognised outputs, level meters on the map, colours, your own test sounds, update checks and the About section.

Making SpeakerWeave the Windows sound output

The Windows volume works as you would expect: the volume keys of a keyboard, the volume wheel of a mouse or headset and the Windows volume slider turn your speakers up and down, and muting Windows mutes them. Follow the Windows volume and mute switches this off.

Make SpeakerWeave the main sound output of Windows is on from the start. While the sound plays, Windows plays into the device SpeakerWeave listens to (SpeakerWeave Virtual Speaker output). If Listen to is still on the test signal, a virtual sound device is picked for you. With Start SpeakerWeave when Windows starts on as well (the installer offers it), this happens each time the PC boots. When the sound stops or SpeakerWeave quits, Windows goes back to the output it used before, unless you switch that off or picked another output yourself in the meantime. Set the virtual device to 7.1 (also on from the start) is what makes games send surround.

Colours

Under Look, pick one of six themes: Midnight (the default), Ember, Forest, Neon, Amber and Daylight (a light one). They were picked with the colour harmony rules of Adobe Color. Your own colours lets you set the background, panels, lines, text and accent yourself. To use a theme from Adobe Color (color.adobe.com), copy its five colour codes and paste them in, or load a theme saved as an .ase file. SpeakerWeave arranges them so text stays readable, and you can change each colour afterwards.

Bluetooth

Bluetooth devices under the output list shows what is paired with the PC and opens the Windows Bluetooth settings for pairing and connecting. Connected Bluetooth speakers and headphones appear in the output list like any other output. Bluetooth adds a noticeable delay, so add Extra delay to the other outputs to line them up.

Frequency limits and subwoofers

Select a speaker and look at Frequency range:

  • Nothing below cuts everything under the given frequency
  • Nothing above cuts everything over the given frequency
  • The slope sets how sharply the cut happens

Giving a speaker the role Subwoofer (LFE) switches both limits on at 10 and 75 Hz for you. Change the numbers, or drag the two orange lines in the graph, to taste. The Small speaker button does the opposite and keeps deep bass away from a tiny speaker.

On the Sound tab, Send the bass of every channel to the subwoofer as well makes the subwoofer useful for stereo music too, not only for the dedicated bass channel of films and games.

Nothing below hands a speaker's lowest notes to the subwoofer. While no subwoofer is playing, those notes would simply be lost and the sound turns thin, so the limit waits until there is one (the Speaker tab says so). No subwoofer playing: every speaker keeps its lows on the Sound tab switches this off.

Equaliser

Each speaker has its own. Drag a dot up or down to boost or cut, left or right to change the frequency, and scroll over it to make it wider or narrower. Exact numbers can be typed below the graph. Double click empty space for a new band, double click a dot to reset it. Copy and Paste carry the equaliser and frequency range from one speaker to another.

Timing

Every speaker plays a little late, and each by a different amount: Bluetooth and TVs add their own lag, and sound takes about 3 ms to travel each metre. The tools under the map line them up so every speaker's sound reaches your seat together.

  • Easy sync walks you through it by ear. Two speakers at a time tick together. The click seems to come from the speaker that plays first, so add delay to that one, a little at a time, until you hear a single click from between the two. Then the next pair, until every speaker has been matched (subwoofers come last, with a low thump instead of a click). At the end, the speaker that needed the least delay is set to 0 ms and the same amount comes off all the others, so everything plays as early as possible. Cancel puts every delay back as it was.
  • Metronome ticks on your speakers: every speaker together (one crisp click means they are in time), one speaker after another round the room (the clicks should be perfectly even), or only the selected one. Change the tempo in its menu. The PC sound waits while it ticks.
  • Line up delays delays the nearer speakers, from the distances on the map, so all of them reach your seat at the same moment. Good as a first step before Easy sync.
  • TVs process sound for a while before playing it, and every model takes a different time. The app cannot measure that. If one screen is ahead of the others (you hear an echo or a doubled sound), select it, open the Output tab and raise Extra delay until the echo closes up. Game mode on the TV usually shortens its lag.
  • The Safety buffer on the Sound tab is extra delay the app adds on purpose. It starts at 0 ms, the least delay possible: SpeakerWeave then keeps only the room your sound cards need to play cleanly (about 20 to 25 ms for most), and adds a few milliseconds by itself if one runs dry. All outputs keep the same delay, so they stay in line. The status line at the bottom shows the delay in use. Raise the buffer only if you still hear crackles.

Lights

RGB hardware is driven through OpenRGB (free, https://openrgb.org), which supports most motherboard headers, LED controllers and RGB peripherals.

  1. Open OpenRGB and check it can see and control your lights.
  2. In OpenRGB go to SDK Server and press Start Server.
  3. In SpeakerWeave open the Lights tab and press Connect.
  4. Select a speaker. In its Lights section pick the light (or the zone of a controller) that belongs to it, an effect and a colour.

Speakers whose lights only connect to their own remote or their own app, and that OpenRGB does not know, cannot be controlled.

Uninstalling

Settings > Uninstall > Uninstall SpeakerWeave removes SpeakerWeave completely. First Windows gets a normal sound output back (the one it used before SpeakerWeave, or one of your speakers), so the PC is never left playing into the virtual device. Then SpeakerWeave closes and its uninstaller removes the program, the shortcuts, the start with Windows entry and the SpeakerWeave plugin in SimHub (close SimHub first). With Also delete my settings, speaker setups, game setups and test sounds ticked, the folder holding them goes too. Setups you saved somewhere else yourself are kept.

Uninstalling from Windows Settings, Apps does the same, and asks about your settings folder. VB-CABLE stays installed, as other apps may use it too.

If something does not work

Start with the Errors page: it names the problem and usually has a button that fixes it.

What has been tested

The mixing, filters, equaliser, delay, clock correction, room geometry and the whole window are covered by automatic tests (Run tests.bat), including a run against simulated sound cards whose clocks drift apart. The Windows specific parts (real WASAPI devices, listening in on an output, OpenRGB hardware) follow the documented interfaces of the libraries but were written without access to your hardware, so expect to report a rough edge or two.
